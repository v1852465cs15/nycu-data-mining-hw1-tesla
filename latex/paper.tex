\begin{abstract}
Buyers and small dealers need comparable asking-price estimates when screening used electric vehicles. We study 3,754 European Tesla advertisements using a supplied 3,002/752 training/test partition. Ordinary least squares predicts price in euros from seven structured inputs, six title-derived trim indicators, and three model-by-registration-year interactions. The engineered model reduces dealer-grouped cross-validation MAE from \eur\GroupA{} to \eur\GroupC{}, and supplied-test MAE from \eur\TestA{} to \eur\TestC{}. A random forest remains better at \eur\TestRF{}. Neither meets our proposed \eur3,000 test-MAE threshold. Ablations isolate the value of each engineered group, and coefficient and case analyses expose sparse-category uncertainty and extreme-listing failures. Because the test set was inspected during earlier development, these are descriptive benchmark results, not a new blind evaluation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Buyers screening used EVs and small dealers setting asking prices need comparable-price references. A Tesla name alone does not identify trim, software or condition. We predict advertised EUR price, not sale price or certified fair value; large gaps should prompt inspection.

Our question is whether listing-time trim flags and model-specific year slopes improve transparent linear estimation beyond raw fields and correlation ranking. We contribute train-only feature analysis, matched ablations with dealer-grouped validation, and uncertainty/failure analysis. The engineered model is linear in its coefficients; we do not claim superiority over the nonlinear baseline.

\section{Task Definition and Data}
One row is an AutoScout24 advertisement. The continuous target is its asking price in EUR. The training range is \eur\PriceMin{}--\PriceMax{}; it defines observed coverage, not a clipping rule. Inputs are first-registration year, mileage (km), reported power (kW), equipment count, model, seller country, assistance label and selected title keywords. All are available when screening a listing with its price hidden. Seller identity, URL, collection order and target-bearing free text are not predictors.

The supplied workbook records collection on 5 October 2026 across 188 result pages in eight European countries. It contains 3,754 listings, with the first 3,002 in training and 752 in testing. We retain this original partition for reproducibility. Search-page order is neither randomized sampling nor longitudinal forecasting; a later-market claim would require a temporal dataset. The collector's listing-ID deduplication does not establish unique physical vehicles.

\input{summary_table}

All seven structured inputs are populated in the supplied workbook, so no additional imputation is fitted. Earlier filling and assistance annotations are inherited, not independently verified. Numeric fields are standardized using the current training fold; categories are one-hot encoded with unknown categories ignored at prediction. We preserve price extremes, because unusually expensive cars need not be erroneous. One training Model Y has year 2001: the main benchmark retains it to match the shared data, and Section 7 reports an exclusion sensitivity. Reported power and equipment counts are seller fields, not certified specifications; equipment lists can include headings. Exterior colour and range are excluded from the primary model; colour is used only in the additional check E1 (Section 8.1).

Univariate numeric $r^2$ and categorical correlation ratio $\eta^2$ place power, model, mileage, year and country above equipment and assistance. These common-scale scores use raw EUR price, not log-price. We test three explicit hypotheses: \textbf{H1}, trim indicators reduce error conditional on structured fields; \textbf{H2}, model-specific year interactions improve on a shared slope; \textbf{H3}, these two groups outperform raw correlation ranking. Their verdicts come from matched ablations in Section 7 and coefficient interpretation in Section 8, not from these plots alone. Additional checks E1--E4 examine exterior colour (an inherited categorical appearance field), country, model and a squared-power term; these are exploratory checks, not replacements for H1--H3.

\subsection{Pipeline and feature groups}

\caption{Prediction pipeline. Every learned transformation uses only its fit partition. The target enters regression fitting and evaluation, never feature construction.}
\label{fig:pipeline}

\textbf{Set A} contains the seven structured fields. \textbf{Set B} retains the top $k$ fields ranked inside each training fold: squared Pearson correlation for numeric fields and between-category/total target sum of squares for nominal fields. Searching $k=1,\ldots,7$, the smallest value within one standard error ($s/\sqrt{5}$) of the lowest CV MAE is $k=\SelectedK{}$, favoring a smaller representation when gains are within fold uncertainty.

\textbf{Set C}, our final OLS representation, keeps all seven raw fields and adds two groups. Six case-insensitive title flags mark Performance/Plaid, Long Range, AWD/Dual Motor, entry trim (RWD/Standard Range), new generation (Highland/Juniper/Plaid/refresh) and HW4. These capture within-model differences that power alone may miss (H1). Flags are mentions, not verified configurations: Plaid activates two flags, and Standard Range does not certify rear-wheel drive. The reproducibility package preserves exact expressions.

Three interactions allow Model S, X and Y to have different registration slopes:
\begin{equation}
u_m=\mathbf{1}(\mathrm{model}=m)(\mathrm{year}-\overline{\mathrm{year}}_{\mathrm{fit}}).
\end{equation}
They address H2. The fit-partition mean prevents validation-dependent centering. Model 3 shares the base slope with rare models; these latter classes cannot support reliable separate slopes. This is a registration-year association, not observed longitudinal depreciation.

\subsection{Linear model and alternatives}
OLS minimizes $\sum_i(y_i-b-\mathbf z_i^T\boldsymbol\beta)^2$. It offers direct EUR effects and a small fixed representation, while interactions allow non-additive raw-input behavior. It has no regularization parameter. The frozen model uses no target logarithm or post-hoc clipping; the separate power-squared check does not change it. Full one-hot fitting uses a least-squares solution; inference uses an equivalent full-rank reference coding. The retained representation has \NumParameters{} coefficients including the intercept.

Ridge is a plausible alternative for correlated features. A training-only dealer-grouped sensitivity searches $\alpha\in\{.01,.1,1,10,100,1000\}$ on the same Set C inputs; the lowest MAE is \eur\RidgeBest{} at $\alpha=\RidgeAlpha{}$, compared with OLS \eur\GroupC{}. We retain the already specified OLS benchmark and report this alternative without choosing a new model from test outcomes. Coefficient uncertainty is conditional on this representation.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\subsection{Validation and provenance}
We use identical five-fold shuffled KFold partitions (seed 42) across methods, plus five-fold GroupKFold by seller/dealer as the structure-aware check. Five folds retain about 80\% for fitting while permitting a spread estimate at modest cost. There are \TrainGroups{} training dealer groups; each missing seller receives its own group. Scaling, encoding, centering and top-$k$ ranking are fitted within folds. We report mean $\pm$ sample SD. Feature-set/k exploration used these training folds, so CV is development evidence, not a nested estimate of the entire selection process.

The supplied test set was already inspected in earlier team analyses. We freeze this integration's model definitions before recomputation and retain all 752 rows, but do not claim a new one-use blind holdout. Dealer grouping is not a vehicle-identity guarantee; overlap and sensitivity checks are reported separately. A newly collected locked test set remains necessary for confirmatory deployment claims.

\subsection{Metrics and baselines}
MAE measures typical absolute EUR error; RMSE penalizes large misses, and $R^2$ describes explained variation. We use MAE $\leq$ \eur3,000 as an assumed benchmark tolerance. No stakeholder cost data were collected: this is neither an inspection fee nor a validated buyer/dealer cost. Pass/fail and within-threshold shares are descriptive, not evidence of economic benefit or an individual guarantee.

Baselines are the \textbf{Trivial Baseline (training mean)}, \textbf{Simple Baseline (mileage-only OLS)}, and \textbf{Strong Baseline (200-tree random forest on Set A, seed 42; no depth limit, default leaf size 1)}. The forest is an inherited fixed flexible comparator, not a tuned optimum. All methods use identical rows and metrics. Paired test-loss gaps use 10,000 dealer-cluster bootstrap resamples (seed 42), keeping both methods' predictions together. Grouped-fold paired $t$-tests have only five overlapping training fits, so their $p$-values are exploratory.

Table~\ref{tab:gaps} reports car-level paired-bootstrap intervals; dealer-cluster intervals are retained as a sensitivity analysis in the repository.

\subsection{Main comparison}
\input{results_table}
Engineered OLS improves over raw OLS but remains behind RF (Table~\ref{tab:results}): A-to-C test MAE falls EUR 578 (13.5\%); RF leads C by EUR 484. A forest can capture local combinations beyond our slopes and flags, although this comparison does not isolate the mechanism. Our final predictor remains linear.

C and RF exceed the EUR 3,000 threshold by EUR 711 and EUR 227. Only 55.6\%/59.4\% of their test errors fall within it. These are inspection aids, not autonomous pricing decisions.

On the 600 non-overlapping test cars, the forest's lead over C shrinks to EUR 215 (3,816 vs 4,031), and it exceeds the threshold by EUR 816; C's gain over A remains EUR 519.

\paragraph{Resolving the hypotheses.}
H1--H3 retain their Section 4 definitions. Removing flags raises KFold/grouped MAE by EUR 213/198; removing interactions raises them by EUR 462/455 (Table~\ref{tab:abl}). C beats B by EUR 723/698 in those validations and EUR 617 on test. These results support H1, H2 and H3 for this benchmark, not prospective or causal claims. A group can help even if an individual coefficient is insignificant.

\paragraph{Additional checks.}
E1--E4 add or remove one group and compare KFold MAE with C (paired fold $t$-test). Colour gives no prediction gain, although a joint Wald test finds a small in-sample link. Country matters, but little (+EUR 78, $p=0.010$). Removing car model, with its year terms, costs EUR 2,149, by far the largest change. Price is curved in power ($-$EUR 107, $p<.001$); we report this without changing the frozen model. A small $p$-value does not make an effect useful: compare its natural-unit size to the EUR 3,000 screening tolerance.

\section{Conclusion and Limitations}
Trim flags and model-specific year slopes reduce test MAE to EUR 3,711, versus EUR 4,290 for raw OLS and EUR 3,227 for RF. H1--H3 are supported within this benchmark, but neither C nor RF meets the assumed EUR 3,000 tolerance. Use estimates to prioritize inspections, verifying trim, battery condition and specifications.

One website, eight countries and one asking-price snapshot limit coverage. The supplied order split, prior test exposure, possible duplicates, inherited annotations and rare classes limit generalization. EUR 3,000 lacks stakeholder cost evidence and is stricter for cheaper cars. Linear extrapolation can be implausible; associations are not causal depreciation. Next, freeze the rules and collect a locked snapshot with vehicle-identity/battery checks, evaluating accuracy and actual screening decisions.
